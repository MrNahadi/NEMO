\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

This chapter establishes the engineering context, defines the problem addressed
by the study, and presents its aim, objectives, significance, and limits. The
three component case studies are introduced together because they provide
different tests of the same optimization and CAD--analysis workflow.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

Marine vessels and offshore systems contain many secondary structural
components, including machinery brackets, lifting points, appendage roots,
stiffeners, doublers, and attachment details. Although small relative to the
supporting vessel or platform, these components transfer concentrated or
distributed loads into a larger structure. Their behaviour can therefore
affect equipment alignment, lifting safety, vibration, watertight integrity,
and the durability of adjacent structure.

The load paths are often three-dimensional. A bracket can combine baseplate
and rib bending, fastener reactions, bearing, contact, and local stress at
fillets or welds. A padeye can combine pin bearing, net-section tension,
shear-out, lug-root stress, gusset action, and weld load transfer. A stabilizer
fin can combine distributed pressure, root bending, shear, torsion, shell
action, local buckling, fatigue, and hydrodynamic effects. A credible design
study must therefore distinguish the behaviour represented by its models from
the failure modes that require later investigation.

Mass reduction is useful because structural mass affects material use, vessel
displacement, payload, installation effort, fuel demand, and
centre-of-gravity management. It cannot, however, be pursued independently of
strength, stiffness, stability, fatigue life, corrosion allowance,
manufacturability, inspection, and damage tolerance. The design task is
therefore formulated as constrained mass minimization.

Traditional development commonly alternates between preliminary calculations,
manual CAD changes, and selected finite element analyses. This approach can
produce acceptable designs, but it examines only a small portion of a
multi-variable design space and can separate dimensions, loads, material data,
boundary conditions, and results into unrelated records. Parametric modelling
offers a more systematic basis for iteration. A family of geometries can be
evaluated, compared, and reduced to a small set of candidates for
higher-fidelity work. Research has connected commercial CAD and CAE tools to
automated optimization loops \cite{park2010}; however, geometric change also
alters the faces used for loads, supports, contacts, and mesh controls. The
engineering meaning of these regions must be preserved, an issue described as
the preservation of simulation intent \cite{nolan2015}.

The Nelder-Mead Marine Optimizer (\NEMO) addresses this problem through a
tiered workflow. First-order analytical models guide design-space exploration
and optimization. Autodesk Fusion then rebuilds native geometry and provides
physical-property evidence for selected candidates. Final FEA remains a
human-reviewed activity because the public Fusion Python API used in this
project did not provide a dependable route to initiate the required Static
Stress solves and extract every required result. The workflow consequently
keeps analytical predictions, CAD properties, and solver evidence as distinct
claims.

The bracket provides the complete proof of concept, from parameter definition

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem-statement}

Manual design iteration does not systematically explore the combined effects
of plate dimensions, stiffener geometry, fillet radius, lug proportions, spar
position, and reinforcement size. Conversely, solving a detailed FEA model
for every candidate would require more time, computation, and software
interaction than a resource-constrained student project can support. A
practical method is needed to use inexpensive models for broad exploration
and reserve manual FEA for a small, traceable candidate set.

The method must also preserve evidence integrity. The engineering definition,
design variables, calculated metrics, generated geometry, load and support
regions, and validation record must remain connected. A generated solid is not
a successful analysis if a required boundary region is absent; a stale CAD
response must not be associated with a new design; CAD mass must not be
presented as structural validation; and a completed solver report must be
rejected when its applied load is incorrect.

The resulting research gap is not a lack of optimization algorithms or CAD
software. It is the need for a compact, inspectable workflow that combines
bounded design-space exploration, failure-contained evaluation, native CAD
generation, semantic boundary identification, and an explicit separation of
analytical, CAD, and FEA evidence on an ordinary engineering workstation.

\section{Aim and Objectives}
\label{sec:objectives}

The project aims to develop and evaluate an auditable, resource-conscious
workflow for the parameterized mass optimization and validation preparation
of marine structural components.

The specific objectives are to:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item define unit-aware parameter spaces, materials, loads, constraints,
    analytical screening models, and boundary roles for a bracket, padeye, and
    stabilizer;
    \item explore each bounded design space using seeded Latin-hypercube
    sampling and scaled, multi-start Nelder--Mead optimization while retaining
    invalid evaluations as controlled records;
    \item generate native Fusion geometry, physical properties, exchange
    artifacts, and semantic boundary metadata through a versioned CAD bridge;
    \item verify each CAD generator at its baseline and 20 seeded design
    vectors, and prepare traceable baseline and finalist validation sets;
    \item evaluate the lower-mass analytical candidates and compare analytical
    mass with native-CAD mass; and
    \item demonstrate the bracket proof of concept and identify the further
    evidence required before any manufacturing or service decision.
\end{enumerate}

These objectives are examined through four questions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Can one part-aware workflow represent and process the three component
    types without embedding bracket-specific assumptions in shared operations?
    \item Can seeded sampling and bounded multi-start search identify
    lower-mass, analytically feasible candidates for all three parts?
    \item Can native CAD and the required load and support regions remain
    usable across bounded geometry changes, and how closely does CAD mass
    agree with the analytical estimates?
    \item What does the bracket proof of concept reveal about the evidence
    required before the candidates can be accepted as structural designs?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Significance of the Study}
\label{sec:significance}

The study demonstrates how low-cost analytical screening can reduce the number
of candidates requiring manual CAD and FEA attention while retaining the
information needed to review each decision. Its contribution is an
implementation-and-evaluation workflow rather than a new optimization
algorithm.

The engineering definitions keep variables, units, bounds, materials, loads,
constraints, and required boundary roles consistent across evaluation,
optimization, candidate selection, and CAD generation. Scaled coordinates,
bounded trials, explicit feasibility filtering, and controlled failed
evaluations prevent one invalid design from ending a search or being promoted
as an acceptable finalist. The CAD handoff records physical properties and
identifies engineering regions such as fixed supports, equipment-load faces,
pin-bearing surfaces, pressure bands, and displacement monitors. These records
do not replace engineering judgement, but they make the intended load path
inspectable.

The workflow is also suitable for an educational setting because assumptions
and evidence levels remain visible. It operates using existing student
hardware, educational software access, open-source Python packages, and local
data exchange. These resources have economic value, but the study required no
new direct project expenditure.

\section{Scope and Limitations}
\label{sec:scope}

The implemented study covers three registered component definitions,
first-order linear-elastic screening, six bracket variables, nine padeye
variables, nine stabilizer variables, seeded sampling, bounded multi-start
optimization, recorded run results, native Fusion generation, STEP and
optional STL export, semantic boundary metadata, CAD sweeps, and baseline and
finalist validation preparation. A results dashboard supports review of the
recorded optimization data but does not provide a separate calculation model.

The analytical models estimate mass, a governing stress, yield-based factor of
safety, and displacement. The bracket uses an equivalent cantilever
representation; the padeye compares net-section, bearing, shear-out, and
lateral-bending estimates; and the stabilizer uses root-section bending and
shear under a prescribed distributed load. The models assume homogeneous,
isotropic, linear-elastic material and declared design envelopes. They do not
resolve detailed contact, fastener preload, weld behaviour, three-dimensional
local stress, nonlinear response, shell buckling, fatigue, corrosion, impact,
vibration, fluid--structure interaction, or hydrodynamic pressure prediction.

The project uses software, analytical, CAD, and FEA evidence as separate
levels. Software tests and analytical results are complete for the reported
scope, and the CAD campaigns provide geometry, mass, artifacts, and
boundary-role evidence. The bracket includes two exploratory solver studies,
but neither supplies acceptable structural results: the second study records
1.472~N rather than the intended 1472~N, while the first does not provide a
complete traceable load definition. Padeye and stabilizer FEA remain future
work.

The finite sample and three local starts do not establish a global optimum.
The defensible optimization claim is the best feasible design found within
the declared parameterized search. The project also does not establish
classification compliance, fabrication readiness, or service safety.
Acceptance would require verified loads and materials, applicable design-code
checks, calibrated and mesh-converged FEA, relevant fatigue, buckling,
corrosion, contact, fastener, and weld assessments, physical testing where
required, and qualified engineering review.
