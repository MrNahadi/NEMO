\section{Chapter Overview}

This chapter introduces the engineering problem, the project aim, the research
questions, and the boundaries of the reported evidence. It also defines the
project contribution and explains why the three selected components provide a
useful test of one shared workflow. The chapter closes with the structure of
the report.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

Marine vessels and offshore systems contain many secondary structural
components. Examples include machinery foundations, equipment brackets,
lifting points, appendage roots, stiffeners, local doublers, and attachment
details. A single component may be small compared with a hull or an offshore
platform, but it can transfer a concentrated load into a much larger
structure. Its behavior can therefore affect equipment alignment, lifting
safety, watertight integrity, vibration, and the durability of adjacent
structure.

The load path through a small component is often three-dimensional. A bracket
can combine baseplate bending, rib bending, fastener reactions, bearing,
contact, and local stress at a fillet or weld. A padeye can combine pin
bearing, net-section tension, shear-out, out-of-plane bending, lug-root
stress, gusset action, and weld load transfer. A stabilizer fin can combine
distributed pressure, root bending, shear, torsion, shell action, spar
behavior, local buckling, fatigue, and hydrodynamic effects. A credible design
process must identify which of these effects the selected model can represent
and which effects remain outside its intended use.

Mass is also an important design quantity. Added structural mass increases
material use and can affect vessel displacement, payload, installation
effort, fuel demand, and center-of-gravity management. However, mass reduction
is not a sufficient design objective on its own. Removing material can reduce
strength, stiffness, stability, fatigue life, corrosion reserve, fabrication
tolerance, and damage tolerance. Marine component design is therefore a
constrained task: mass can be reduced only while applicable strength,
displacement, stability, fatigue, manufacturing, inspection, and service
requirements remain satisfied.

Traditional component development often moves between preliminary
calculations, manual CAD edits, and selected FEA studies. This process can
produce an acceptable design, but it normally examines only a small part of a
multi-variable design space. The reasoning can also become fragmented.
Dimensions may be held in a drawing, loads in a calculation sheet, materials
in a simulation setup, boundary conditions in screenshots, and candidate
history in personal notes. When the evidence becomes separated, a reviewer
cannot easily reproduce the result or determine whether one image belongs to
the same design as one table of values.

Parametric modeling provides a different basis for iteration. A parametric
model represents a family of related designs rather than one fixed geometry.
An optimization process can change the dimensions, evaluate each candidate,
record its performance, and select candidates for more detailed work.
Established research has connected commercial CAD and CAE tools into
automated optimization loops \cite{park2010}. This integration can reduce
repetitive work, but it introduces a difficult problem: a geometry change can
also change the topology used for loads, supports, contacts, and mesh
controls. The model must preserve the engineering meaning of these regions,
not only rebuild a visually plausible solid. Nolan \emph{et al.} describe this
need as the preservation of simulation intent \cite{nolan2015}.

\section{Project Context}

The Nelder-Mead Marine Optimizer (\NEMO) was developed in response to the
combined problems of design-space exploration, traceability, and CAD--analysis
handoff. The external Python application stores part definitions, evaluates
analytical models, samples the design space, runs the optimizer, records
results, and creates validation packages. The Autodesk Fusion add-in rebuilds
native CAD, calculates physical properties, exports neutral geometry, and
describes the faces associated with engineering boundary roles.

The original proof-of-concept plan targeted a fully automatic cycle in which
the optimizer would request a design, Fusion would rebuild the CAD and solve a
Static Stress study, and the structural results would return to the optimizer.
An implementation investigation found that the public Fusion Python API used
by this project did not provide a dependable route to start the required
Static Stress solves and extract all required result quantities. The project
therefore changed to a tiered workflow. Low-cost analytical models guide the
search, Fusion produces native CAD and mass evidence for selected designs, and
an engineer prepares and checks final FEA studies manually.

This change reduced the degree of automation, but it created a clearer
evidence boundary. A CAD-only response contains volume, mass, and artifact
paths. It leaves stress, factor of safety, and displacement empty. The
reserved open-source FEA mode also returns a controlled failure because no
Gmsh and CalculiX implementation currently exists. The software does not
invent values when an evaluator is unavailable.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem-statement}

This project addressed two related problems.

The first problem concerned engineering efficiency. Manual iteration does not
systematically explore the combined effects of plate size, stiffener
dimensions, fillet radius, lug geometry, spar position, and reinforcement
dimensions. A high-fidelity FEA solve for every candidate would require more
time, software interaction, and computation than a resource-constrained
student project could support. A practical method was needed to use inexpensive
models for broad exploration and reserve manual FEA for a small, traceable
candidate set.

The second problem concerned evidence integrity. A useful workflow must
preserve the connection between the engineering definition, design variables,
calculated metrics, generated geometry, load and support regions, and
validation record. A geometry generator must not report success when a
required boundary region is missing. A stale response must not be attached to
a new design. A CAD mass must not be presented as proof of structural
performance. A solver report must not be accepted when its input load is
incorrect.

These problems lead to a narrow research gap. The methods used by the project
are established, but they are rarely presented together as a small,
inspectable reference workflow that works on an ordinary engineering
workstation, supports several marine component archetypes, contains failure,
and separates analytical, CAD, and FEA evidence.

\section{Aim}

The project aimed to develop and evaluate an auditable, resource-conscious
workflow for the parameterized mass optimization and validation preparation
of marine structural components.

\section{Specific Objectives}
\label{sec:objectives}

The specific objectives were to:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item define part parameters, units, bounds, baselines, materials, loads,
    constraints, fixed geometry, and semantic boundary roles in inspectable
    source files;
    \item develop first-order analytical models for mass, stress,
    yield-based factor of safety, and displacement;
    \item explore each bounded design space with seeded
    Latin-hypercube sampling;
    \item implement a scaled, bounded, multi-start Nelder--Mead search;
    \item preserve invalid candidates and unavailable evaluators as controlled
    failed records instead of terminating an optimization campaign;
    \item implement a versioned file protocol between the external Python
    application and an Autodesk Fusion add-in;
    \item generate native Fusion geometry, STEP files, optional STL files,
    physical properties, and semantic boundary metadata;
    \item test each native CAD generator over its baseline and 20 seeded design
    vectors;
    \item prepare baseline and finalist packages for manual Fusion Static
    Stress studies;
    \item demonstrate the complete intended method with the bracket proof of
    concept;
    \item show how the same software architecture extends to a lifting padeye
    and stabilizer fin; and
    \item state the engineering evidence required before any manufacturing or
    service decision.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:research-questions}

The study was guided by six questions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Can one part-aware software architecture represent and process a
    bracket, padeye, and stabilizer without bracket-only assumptions in shared
    workflow code?
    \item Can seeded Latin-hypercube sampling and multi-start bounded
    Nelder--Mead search find lower-mass, analytically feasible candidates for
    all three parts?
    \item Can the Fusion bridge rebuild valid native CAD and identify every
    required semantic boundary role across bounded geometry changes?
    \item How closely do analytical mass estimates agree with the Fusion CAD
    mass for each baseline and selected finalist?
    \item What evidence is still required before the analytical candidates can
    be treated as validated structural designs?
    \item What lessons does the bracket proof of concept provide for future
    padeye and stabilizer FEA studies?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Research Contribution}
\label{sec:contribution}

The contribution is an implementation-and-evaluation contribution, not a new
optimization algorithm. It has four main parts.

\subsection{Part-Aware Engineering Definitions}

Each component is defined by one unit-aware manifest. The manifest contains
exact parameter names, declared units, lower and upper bounds, baseline
values, material properties, loads, constraints, fixed geometry, and semantic
boundary roles. Evaluation, optimization, logging, validation packaging, and
Fusion generation use the same definition. This design reduces the risk that
one layer applies a different load, bound, or unit.

\subsection{Failure-Contained Optimization}

The optimizer scales each variable to the interval from zero to one, clips
trial points at the bounds, applies explicit penalties, caches repeated
evaluations, and records every unique evaluation. Invalid candidates and
unavailable evaluators return a controlled high objective. One bad candidate
does not terminate the complete search. The final candidate selection filters
explicitly for feasibility instead of assuming that the smallest penalized
objective must pass every constraint.

\subsection{Inspectable CAD Bridge}

The bridge separates two Python environments. The external environment can
use the full project dependency set but cannot directly run inside Fusion.
The Fusion environment has access to the native design API but should not rely
on external scientific packages. Versioned JSON request and response files
connect the environments. Atomic replacement reduces partial-read risk, while
the run identifier and iteration prevent a stale response from being accepted
as a new result.

\subsection{Semantic Boundary Evidence}

Each generated CAD model identifies engineering regions such as
\code{fixed\_support}, \code{equipment\_load}, \code{pin\_bearing},
\code{pressure\_band\_1}, and \code{tip\_monitor}. The metadata records
geometric selectors and face signatures. This method does not guarantee
permanent CAD face identity, but it makes the intended load and support
meaning explicit and gives the engineer a checkable starting point for FEA.

\section{Significance of the Study}
\label{sec:significance}

The work has engineering, educational, and software significance. From an
engineering perspective, the workflow can reduce the number of designs that
require manual CAD and FEA attention. It also shows where a low-cost model is
adequate for ranking and where higher-fidelity work is necessary.

From an educational perspective, the workflow makes hidden assumptions
visible. A student can inspect the material, load derivation, bounds,
equations, penalties, request files, response files, and validation
checklists. The system therefore supports discussion of model credibility,
not only the display of an optimized shape.

From a software perspective, the project shows how a small system can remain
part-aware across the registry, analytical evaluation, optimizer, logger,
dashboard, bridge, and CAD generator. It also shows the value of returning a
controlled partial result. The absence of a solver result remains visible
instead of being hidden behind a successful CAD operation.

The zero-cash-cost execution is also important. The project used existing
student hardware, educational software access, open-source Python packages,
local file exchange, and university supervision. This arrangement makes the
method accessible without a cloud-computing budget. It does not imply that
the hardware, software access, electricity, internet, supervision, and
student labor have no economic value. Chapter~\ref{chap:planning} distinguishes
direct expenditure from these in-kind resources.

\section{Scope}
\label{sec:scope}

\subsection{Implemented Software Scope}

The implemented scope contains:

\begin{itemize}
    \item three registered part definitions;
    \item first-order linear-elastic screening models;
    \item six continuous bracket variables and nine variables for each of the
    padeye and stabilizer;
    \item mass minimization with factor-of-safety and displacement penalties;
    \item random and Latin-hypercube sampling;
    \item bounded multi-start Nelder--Mead optimization;
    \item CSV and JSON run records;
    \item a Streamlit results dashboard;
    \item native Fusion generators for all three parts;
    \item STEP and optional STL export;
    \item semantic boundary metadata;
    \item baseline plus 20-vector CAD generator sweeps;
    \item baseline plus five-finalist validation packages; and
    \item a bracket Static Stress setup demonstration.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Engineering Scope}

The analytical models estimate mass, a governing stress, yield-based factor of
safety, and displacement. The bracket model uses an equivalent cantilever
representation. The padeye model compares net-section, bearing, shear-out,
and lateral-bending stress estimates. The stabilizer model uses a root-section
bending and shear representation under a prescribed distributed load.

These models support screening and ranking. They do not resolve local
three-dimensional stress, fastener preload, weld detail, pin contact,
geometric or material nonlinearity, shell buckling, fatigue damage, corrosion,
impact, vibration, fluid--structure interaction, or hydrodynamic pressure
prediction.

\subsection{Evidence Scope}

The report uses four evidence levels:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{software evidence}, from automated offline tests and
    controlled command-line runs;
    \item \textbf{analytical evidence}, from the declared first-order models;
    \item \textbf{CAD evidence}, from native geometry, mass, volume, exports,
    and boundary-role sweeps; and
    \item \textbf{FEA evidence}, which requires checked inputs, equilibrium,
    mesh convergence, and correctly recorded solver outputs.
\end{enumerate}

Only the first three levels are complete for all parts. The bracket has a
manual FEA setup report, but its saved load is incorrect by a factor of one
thousand. Its numerical solver results are therefore excluded from structural
acceptance.

\section{Assumptions}
\label{sec:assumptions}

The principal assumptions are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item the declared material properties represent homogeneous, isotropic,
    linear-elastic material during screening;
    \item the bracket design envelope uses a 50~kg equipment mass and a
    dynamic amplification factor of 3;
    \item the padeye design envelope uses a 500~kg working mass and a dynamic
    amplification factor of 3;
    \item the stabilizer load uses a prescribed lift coefficient, planform
    area, speed, seawater density, dynamic factor, and spanwise load
    distribution;
    \item the declared parameter bounds prevent most grossly invalid geometry
    but do not prove manufacturability;
    \item the factor-of-safety constraint uses material yield strength and does
    not represent fatigue or buckling;
    \item the displacement limits are project criteria, not class-approved
    service limits; and
    \item the native CAD mass provides a geometry consistency check but does
    not validate stress or displacement.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations and Delimitations}
\label{sec:intro-limitations}

The study does not claim a global optimum. Nelder--Mead is a local method, the
number of starts is finite, and a 60-point sample remains sparse in a
nine-dimensional space. The defensible claim is the best feasible design
found within the declared parameterized search.

The project does not complete a formal multi-fidelity optimization loop.
Analytical predictions are not automatically corrected by FEA. The current
workflow is tiered: analytical screening comes first, CAD verification comes
second, and manual FEA comes last.

The project does not establish classification compliance, statutory
compliance, fabrication readiness, or service safety. A later design stage
must select the governing rules and load combinations, verify material and
fabrication data, perform calibrated FEA, address fatigue, buckling,
corrosion, contact, fasteners, and welds, and obtain qualified engineering
review.

\section{Safety and Claim Boundary}
\label{sec:claim-boundary}

Every analytical value in this report is a screening estimate. A design with
a reported factor of safety above 2.5 has passed the implemented yield-based
screening equation. It has not passed all relevant marine failure modes. A
successful CAD generation result proves only that the requested geometry and
artifacts were produced under the generator acceptance checks.

A Fusion study is not credible only because it solved. The material, load,
support, contacts, mesh, equilibrium, result location, and convergence must
also be correct. This principle is demonstrated by the bracket report: Fusion
completed a solve, but inspection found that the study used 1.472~N instead of
1472~N. The project rejects those numerical results.
